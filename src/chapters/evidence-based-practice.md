# Introduction to Evidence-Based Practice (EBP)

Evidence-Based Practice (EBP) is a cornerstone in modern healthcare. It is a system that guides practitioners to make decisions grounded in the best available, current, valid, and relevant evidence. Dr David Sackett, a pioneer in EBP, defined it as the "conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of the individual patient". This practice requires integrating external clinical evidence with individual clinical expertise. Importantly, EBP also emphasises the inclusion of the patients' expectations, concerns, and values.

## The Importance of EBP

- EBP reduces inconsistency in practice and increases both efficiency and effectiveness.
- It has been accepted by government and funding bodies as an essential component for providing better healthcare.
- The practice promotes an attitude of inquiry among health professionals.
- It encourages practitioners to continuously think about why they do things in a certain way and whether there is evidence to guide them to a more effective method.

## The 5 Steps of Evidence-Based Practice

Evidence-based practice is seen as comprising five explicit steps:

**1. ASK: Formulating an Answerable Clinical Question**

- Asking a specific and focused question is an essential first step.
- The PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) is widely used to structure these questions.
- Other tools like PICOT, SPICE, and ECLIPSE cater to different types of research problems.
- An example question is: "Do patients undergoing lumbar punctures need four hours' bed rest to minimise the risk of post-lumbar puncture headaches?".

**2. ACQUIRE: Obtaining the Best Available Evidence**

- Not all research is equally valuable or useful.
- A clinically useful research study should address a real and important problem, add substantially to existing knowledge, feature a pragmatic design, and measure patient-relevant outcomes.
- It must also provide good value for money, be feasible and acceptable in the real world, and make its data available for verification.
- To find the best evidence, practitioners use the Hierarchy of Evidence, which ranks the relative strength of results obtained from scientific research.
- This hierarchy typically takes the shape of a pyramid:

- **Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses:** These sit at the top of the pyramid and synthesise data from multiple studies.
- **Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs):** Located below systematic reviews, these involve randomly allocating participants into experimental and control groups.
- **Cohort Studies:** Observational studies where participants with a specific condition or treatment are followed over time and compared with a similar unaffected group.
- **Case-Control Studies:** Observational studies comparing two groups that differ in outcome based on a supposed causal attribute.
- **Cross-Sectional Surveys/Studies:** Observational studies analysing data from a population at a specific point in time.
- **Case Reports and Series:** Reports focusing on the treatment of individual patients.

**3. APPRAISE: Critically Appraising the Evidence**

- Not all published research is of sufficient quality to inform clinical decision-making.
- Critical appraisal is a systematic process to evaluate research validity, impact, and applicability.
- **Validity:** Assesses if the methods used were valid and if you can trust the study.
- **Impact:** Evaluates if the study's results and findings are informative, clinically important, and relevant.
- **Applicability:** Determines if these findings can be applied to your patient within a practice setting.

**4. APPLY: Integrating Evidence with Clinical Expertise and Patient Preference**

- Successful EBP involves integrating the newly appraised evidence with your own clinical expertise.
- This step ensures that the best available evidence aligns with the unique values, preferences, and circumstances of individual patients.

**5. ASSESS / EVALUATE: Reflecting on the Process**

- Once implemented, the new practice or change should be evaluated to determine if it should be accepted, rejected, or modified to suit a specific setting.
- Reflection on the EBP process is vital; practitioners should assess the efficiency of each step to identify continuous opportunities for improvement.
- Evaluation helps compare the effects of the intervention with a baseline or other practice areas where it wasn't implemented.
- It provides useful information about the implementation process, identifies staff training and development needs, and highlights new issues to explore in future EBP cycles.

## Factors Influencing Evidence-Based Practice

The implementation of EBP is highly important but equally challenging. Several factors influence its success:

- **Cost Implications:** EBP may incur costs due to changes in service delivery, making a well-defined budget for staff, equipment, and resources crucial.
- **Time:** Developing a timeline for project activities ensures timely completion.
- **Resources:** Identifying and securing the necessary resources is essential for successful implementation.
- **Outcome Measures:** Identifying short- and long-term indicators of success, as well as ways to collect relevant data, is critical.
- **Support Groups:** Finding individuals or groups that support the change is crucial for overcoming resistance to the new practice.
- **Strategies for Change:** Implementation requires active strategies like building knowledge, creating awareness, promoting action, and pursuing sustainability.

## Clinical Audit

- A clinical audit is a process that provides a systematic framework for establishing care standards based on the best evidence.
- It helps identify specific areas of clinical care that require improvement.
- Audits are directed at maintaining and achieving quality in healthcare, aiming to improve patient outcomes and ensure the cost-effective use of resources.
- The audit process utilises multiple methods such as clinical case reviews, document searching, analysis of retained data, and data collection through focus groups or questionnaires.
